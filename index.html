How to use this app

Three steps to a reading. Everything runs inside this page — your recordings never leave the machine.
  1. Drop in your recordings. Drag an audio file onto slot A and another onto slot B — the same riff played on two guitars. WAV, FLAC, AIFF, MP3 or M4A; the sample rate is read from the file, so there is nothing to set. One file on its own is fine too — you get the full single-guitar analysis, just without the comparison views.
  2. Set the tuning you actually played. Top bar, next to A4. The open-string axis and the anatomy regions are derived from it, so Drop D read as E standard puts the landmarks in the wrong place. Leave A4 at 440 Hz unless you deliberately tuned elsewhere.
  3. Keep Level-match on while comparing. It cancels the broadband loudness difference between the two takes, so what you see is tone and not output. It switches itself on when the second file loads. Turn it off only when the level gap itself is the question.

Then just read down the page

  • At a glance — the difference in a sentence or two.
  • Frequency analysis — the spectrum of each guitar, their difference, and the band-energy table. This is the heart of it.
  • Everything below folds open when you want it: tone character, EQ match, spectrograms, envelopes.

Two habits worth having

  • Anything you don't recognise is clickable — labels, region names, the dots on the plots. Each one opens what it measures, with your numbers in the formula.
  • Haven't recorded yet? How to record a fair comparison matters more than anything in this app: change only the guitar.

About Claude Rameau

This app began as a simple question about two guitars.

I own a Les Paul and an SG with the same pickups, the same strings, the same tuning — everything identical except their bodies. I wanted to measure what my ears claimed to hear. So I built a spectrum analyzer with an AI collaborator, and while adding a small feature — overlaying each string's fundamental and harmonics on the spectrum — I noticed something I wasn't looking for: one string's harmonic landed exactly on another string's fundamental. Not close. Exact.

Pulling that thread led to the discovery that the 3rd and 5th notes of a major chord are literally harmonics of the root — that a chord is what one string is already quietly doing by itself. Then further: why some notes rest and others pull, why a scale is three interlocking families, why the physics of a vibrating string contains most of what we call music theory. None of it was invented. It was all sitting there, in the ratios, waiting — the same bedrock Pythagoras hit with a monochord, Rameau hit with a harpsichord in 1722, and I hit with an FFT and a Les Paul.

The app is named for Jean-Philippe Rameau, the composer who first insisted harmony was physics — and for Claude, the AI I built it with. Rameau's brother was also named Claude; the family made a tradition of standing beside the material and asking questions of it.

That's what this tool is for. It will show you the difference between two guitars. But its real hope is that somewhere in the overlays, you'll see a harmonic land exactly where it shouldn't be a coincidence — and ask the only question that matters: yes, but why does it sound that way?

How to record a fair comparison

The one rule: change only the guitar. Every difference this tool shows you is a difference between the two recordings — it can only speak for the guitars if the rest of the chain (player, pick, room, mic, cable, levels, settings) is identical.

Signal chain — pick one and keep it

  • Electric, direct (recommended): guitar → cable → audio interface instrument (Hi-Z) input. No amp, no pedals, no amp sim. This is the cleanest reading of the instrument itself. One cable, same input, same gain knob for both takes.
  • Electric, mic'd amp: only if the amp's character is part of the question. Same amp, same settings, mic untouched between takes — even a centimeter of mic movement shifts the spectrum more than most guitar swaps. Never compare a DI take against a mic'd take.
  • Acoustic: one microphone on a stand (not handheld), 30–60 cm away, aimed where the neck meets the body. Mark your seat and the mic position; re-seat yourself exactly for the second guitar. Aiming at the soundhole exaggerates the body's air resonance and flatters whichever guitar is closer to the mic.

Levels

  • Set the input gain once, with the louder guitar: strum hard, aim the meter around −12 dB with peaks safely under 0 — then don't touch it for the other take.
  • If clipping happens, lower the gain and redo both takes. A clipped take is unusable.
  • Different pickup outputs are fine — the Level-match switch in the top bar removes broadband level differences in the analysis; distortion cannot be removed.

What to play

  • The same riff, same tempo, same pick, same picking position, ~10–30 seconds. Something with open chords and a few single notes covers the range well.
  • Record each take as one continuous performance — don't splice.
  • A second of silence at the start is fine; onset detection ignores it.

What to avoid

  • No compression, EQ, reverb, gates or noise reduction anywhere in the chain — they reshape exactly the spectrum you're trying to measure.
  • Avoid heavily lossy exports if you can; WAV or FLAC beats a low-bitrate MP3. If you must use lossy, use it for both files.
  • Don't normalize or edit the files afterwards — analyze what you recorded.

File handling

Drop the two files above — sample rate and bit depth are read from the file itself, and the two files don't need to match each other. Everything runs locally in this page; nothing is uploaded. One file alone gets the full single-guitar analysis.
